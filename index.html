    Where does 9/4 come from?

    On October 2, 2026, OpenAI posted a short proof. It says that two big square tables of numbers (matrices) can be multiplied with roughly \(n^{9/4} = n^{2.25}\) steps. (More exactly: \(n^{2.25 + \varepsilon}\) steps, for any tiny extra \(\varepsilon\).) The record before that was about \(n^{2.371}\), and it had moved very slowly for 36 years.

    This page explains where the number 9/4 comes from. We start from school algebra and build every idea we need. You can play with each idea, and short quizzes let you check yourself. Plan for about an hour. You need exponents, polynomials, sums written with \(\sum\), a little about logarithms, and the basics of complex numbers (\(a + bi\)).

    The whole proof in five steps
    1. We measure the speed of matrix multiplication with one number, called ω (the Greek letter omega).
    2. We build “meters” that give a difficulty score to any multiplication task. A theorem of Volker Strassen says this: if every meter gives matrix multiplication a low score, then a fast method exists.
    3. We point the meters at a different task: multiplying two polynomials. That task is cheap, so its score has a ceiling.
    4. A new machine from the paper gives two new rules. These rules show that the same score must also grow fast. That gives a floor.
    5. If a meter scored matrix multiplication above \(n^{9/4}\), the floor would rise above the ceiling. That is impossible. So every meter scores at most \(n^{9/4}\), and \(\omega \le 9/4\).

    Do not worry if these steps mean little now. Each chapter explains one piece. At the end, we come back to this list, and every word in it will make sense.

    Chapter 1

    Multiplying matrices the school way

    A matrix is a square grid of numbers. When we multiply two matrices \(A\) and \(B\), we get a third matrix \(C\). Each entry of \(C\) uses one row of A and one column of B. We multiply the matching pairs and add the results.

    For \(n \times n\) matrices, the answer has \(n^2\) entries. Each entry needs \(n\) multiplications. So the school method uses

    \[ n \times n \times n = n^3 \text{ multiplications.} \]

    That grows fast. If you double \(n\), the work grows 8 times. Computers multiply huge matrices all the time: in graphics, in physics simulations, and inside every modern AI model. So people want to know: what is the best possible growth?

    Chapter 2

    Strassen’s seven

    Here is a warm-up. To multiply two complex numbers, you can write

    \[ (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,i. \]

    That uses 4 multiplications: \(ac\), \(bd\), \(ad\), \(bc\). Carl Friedrich Gauss saw that 3 are enough. Compute

    \[ k_1 = c(a + b), \quad k_2 = a(d - c), \quad k_3 = b(c + d). \]

    Then the real part is \(k_1 - k_3\) and the imaginary part is \(k_1 + k_2\). Check it yourself with the algebra. We used more additions and fewer multiplications. That trade is the main idea of this whole subject.

    Seven products for a 2×2 matrix

    In 1969, Volker Strassen found the same kind of trick for 2×2 matrices. The school method needs 8 multiplications. Strassen’s method needs 7. Press the button to try it with new numbers.

    Why one saved multiplication matters

    Saving 1 out of 8 looks small. The power comes from recursion. A 4×4 matrix is a 2×2 matrix whose entries are 2×2 blocks. Strassen’s formulas still work on blocks. (They never swap the order of a product, so they work even when the “numbers” are matrices.) So a 4×4 product needs 7 block products. Each block product is a 2×2 product, which needs 7. That is \(7 \times 7 = 49\) instead of \(64\).

    For matrices of size \(n = 2^k\), Strassen uses \(7^k\) multiplications. We want this as a power of \(n\). Since \(7 = 2^{2.807\ldots}\), we get \(7^k = (2^k)^{2.807} = n^{2.807}\). The exponent dropped from 3 to about 2.807.

    The recursion rule

    Suppose you can multiply \(u \times u\) matrices with \(r\) multiplications. Then you can multiply \(n \times n\) matrices with about \(n^{e}\) multiplications, where

    \[ e = \log_u r \quad\text{(the power that turns } u \text{ into } r\text{).} \]

    Additions cost something too. At the top level, Strassen adds and subtracts \(n/2 \times n/2\) blocks a fixed number of times. That is about \(n^2\) steps. So the total work \(W(n)\) follows the pattern \(W(n) = 7\,W(n/2) + c\,n^2\) for some fixed number \(c\). If you unroll this pattern, all the \(n^2\) parts add up to at most a constant times \(n^{2.807}\), because \(n^2\) grows slower than \(n^{2.807}\). So the additions change only the constant in front, and the exponent stays the same. From now on, we only count multiplications. Multiplying by a fixed number (like 2 or −1) is free for the same reason.

    Definition

    The exponent of matrix multiplication, \(\omega\), is the smallest number with this property: for every tiny extra amount \(\varepsilon > 0\), you can multiply \(n \times n\) matrices with about \(n^{\omega + \varepsilon}\) steps.

    We know \(\omega \ge 2\), because the answer alone has \(n^2\) numbers to write down. We know \(\omega \le 3\) from the school method. The new theorem says \(\omega \le 9/4\).

    Look at the calculator again. To reach exponent 2.25 with \(u = 2\), you would need \(r \le 2^{2.25} \approx 4.76\). That is impossible, because 2×2 matrices need at least 7. So the recipes behind 9/4 must use huge blocks. Nobody has written one down. The new proof shows that such recipes exist without showing one. You will see how a proof can do that.

    Chapter 3

    Tasks as 3D shapes

    To reason about all possible recipes, we need a way to describe a multiplication task as a single object. Here is the trick. List every product the task needs, and add a label that says where each product goes.

    Call the entries of \(A\) by the letters \(\X{x_{ij}}\), and the entries of \(B\) by \(\Y{y_{jk}}\). The product \(\X{x_{ij}}\Y{y_{jk}}\) goes into the answer at row \(i\), column \(k\). We mark that place with a label \(\Z{z_{ki}}\). Then the whole task is one long sum:

    \[ \MM(n) = \sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n} \X{x_{ij}}\,\Y{y_{jk}}\,\Z{z_{ki}}. \]

    Each term says: “multiply this x by this y, and send the result to this z.” Think of \(\Z{z_{ki}}\) as a tag. Collect all the terms that carry the tag \(\Z{z_{ki}}\). What is left is \(x_{i1}y_{1k} + x_{i2}y_{2k} + \cdots + x_{in}y_{nk}\), which is exactly entry \((i,k)\) of the answer. So the tags sort the products into their places. (We write the label as \(z_{ki}\) and not \(z_{ik}\) on purpose. The indices then run in a cycle \(i \to j \to k \to i\), and that symmetry helps later.)

    Mathematicians call such an object a tensor. We will call it a task. It has three groups of variables, called the three legs:

    X leg: first inputY leg: second inputZ leg: output labels

    Now picture it. Put the X variables along one axis, the Y variables along another, and the Z variables along a third. For each term, place a small cube at its position. A task becomes a 3D shape.

    Rank: the true cost of a task

    One multiplication in a recipe has this form: (some mix of x’s) times (some mix of y’s), and the result is sent to some mix of z’s. For example, Strassen’s \(M_1 = (A_{11} + A_{22})(B_{11} + B_{22})\) is used in \(C_{11}\) and \(C_{22}\). As a piece of the task, it is

    \[ (\X{x_{11} + x_{22}})\,(\Y{y_{11} + y_{22}})\,(\Z{z_{11} + z_{22}}). \]

    The rank of a task is the smallest number of such pieces that add up to exactly the task. Why does this match recipes? A recipe makes products \(M_1, \ldots, M_r\), and then writes each answer entry as a mix of them. Put these mixes into the big sum. Each product \(M_\ell\) then appears once, times the mix of tags of the entries that use it. That is one piece. So a recipe with \(r\) products gives \(r\) pieces, and the reverse also works. Rank is the number of multiplications in the best possible recipe. Strassen showed that \(\MM(2)\) has rank at most 7. (It is exactly 7.)

    Two ways to combine tasks

    Side by side (written \(\oplus\)): two tasks with completely separate variables on all three legs. It is like doing two unrelated jobs. We write the number \(m\) for the task “\(m\) separate single products”.

    Together (written \(\otimes\)): we run two tasks at once and pair up their variables. Each variable of the new task is a pair (one from each task), and each term is a pair of terms. The 3D shape of \(A \otimes B\) is the shape of \(A\) with every cube replaced by a small copy of \(B\). Example: \(\MM(2) \otimes \MM(2) = \MM(4)\). This is the block picture from Chapter 2: a 4×4 product is a 2×2 product of 2×2 blocks. In general, recipes with \(r\) and \(r'\) products for \(A\) and \(B\) give a recipe with \(r \cdot r'\) products for \(A \otimes B\).

    The dot product, and why matrix multiplication is three of them

    The simplest interesting task is the dot product:

    \[ \Dot(m) = \X{x}\,(\Y{y_1}\Z{z_1} + \Y{y_2}\Z{z_2} + \cdots + \Y{y_m}\Z{z_m}). \]

    As a recipe, \(\Dot(m)\) multiplies one number \(x\) by each of \(y_1, \ldots, y_m\), and sends each result to its own tag. That takes \(m\) multiplications, and its rank is exactly \(m\). The name comes from the Y and Z legs, which pair up like a dot product \(y_1 z_1 + \cdots + y_m z_m\). (This version has its lone variable on the X leg. There are two more versions, with the lone variable on the Y leg or on the Z leg.)

    Look again at \(\X{x_{ij}}\Y{y_{jk}}\Z{z_{ki}}\). Each index appears on exactly two legs:

    • index \(j\) is on X and Y, and Z does not see it;
    • index \(k\) is on Y and Z, and X does not see it;
    • index \(i\) is on Z and X, and Y does not see it.

    Each index works like a dot product between two legs. Index \(k\) pairs Y with Z, and X is the lone leg, so it gives \(\Dot_X\). In the same way, index \(i\) gives \(\Dot_Y\), and index \(j\) gives \(\Dot_Z\). When we run the three together with \(\otimes\), each variable collects one index from each dot product where it is not the lone leg. For example, x collects \(i\) and \(j\), which is why it is called \(x_{ij}\). So matrix multiplication is three dot products run together, one for each pair of legs:

    \[ \MM(n) = \Dot_{\X{X}}(n) \otimes \Dot_{\Y{Y}}(n) \otimes \Dot_{\Z{Z}}(n). \]

    Here \(\Dot_{X}\) means the version whose lone variable is on the X leg. Keep this fact in mind. It is where the 3 in \(9/4 = 3 \times 3/4\) comes from.

    Chapter 4

    Multiplying polynomials is cheap

    The proof never works with matrix multiplication directly. It studies a simpler task: multiplying two polynomials. Take

    \[ f(s) = \X{f_0} + \X{f_1}s + \cdots + \X{f_{a-1}}s^{a-1}, \qquad g(s) = \Y{g_0} + \Y{g_1}s + \cdots + \Y{g_{b-1}}s^{b-1}. \]

    So \(f\) has \(a\) coefficients and \(g\) has \(b\) coefficients. Their product has \(a + b - 1\) coefficients. The coefficient of \(s^m\) collects every product \(f_i g_j\) with \(i + j = m\). In the task, we rename \(f_i\) as \(\X{x_i}\) and \(g_j\) as \(\Y{y_j}\), and the tag \(\Z{z_m}\) marks the coefficient of \(s^m\):

    \[ \Poly(a, b) = \sum_{i=0}^{a-1} \sum_{j=0}^{b-1} \X{x_i}\,\Y{y_j}\,\Z{z_{i+j}}. \]

    The direct way uses \(a \times b\) multiplications, one for each pair. But there is a much cheaper way.

    The evaluation trick

    Two points fix a line. Three points fix a parabola. In general, \(a + b - 1\) points fix a polynomial with \(a + b - 1\) coefficients. So we can:

    1. Pick \(a + b - 1\) numbers \(p\). Compute \(f(p)\) and \(g(p)\). This uses only additions and multiplications by fixed numbers, so it is free.
    2. Multiply \(f(p) \cdot g(p)\) for each point. That is \(a + b - 1\) real multiplications.
    3. Rebuild the product polynomial from its values. This is called interpolation, and it is again free. Example: a line \(c_0 + c_1 s\) has the value \(c_0\) at \(s = 0\) and \(c_0 + c_1\) at \(s = 1\). So \(c_0\) is the first value, and \(c_1\) is the second value minus the first. For fixed points, every coefficient is always the same fixed mix of the values.

    This works for any \(a\) and \(b\). So

    \[ \text{rank of } \Poly(a,b) \le a + b - 1. \]

    (It is exactly \(a + b - 1\), but the proof only needs “at most”.)

    For two polynomials of the same length \(a\), the cost is \(2a - 1\). It grows like a straight line in \(a\). Remember this. It will become the ceiling in the final squeeze.

    One more small fact: \(\Poly(1, b) = \X{x_0}(\Y{y_0}\Z{z_0} + \cdots + \Y{y_{b-1}}\Z{z_{b-1}})\). That is exactly \(\Dot(b)\). A polynomial with one coefficient is just a number, and multiplying it into \(g\) is a dot product.

    Chapter 5

    Squishing one task into another

    Suppose you have a recipe for task \(A\). Now replace each x by some mix of new x’s, each y by a mix of new y’s, and each z by a mix of new z’s (a mix may also be zero). Suppose the result is task \(B\). A piece (mix of x’s)(mix of y’s)(mix of z’s) is still a piece of the same form after the replacement. So \(r\) pieces for \(A\) become \(r\) pieces for \(B\), and your recipe for \(A\) also solves \(B\). So \(B\) is no harder than \(A\). We write \(A \ge B\) and call this a squish. (The paper calls it a restriction.)

    Example: in \(\MM(n)\), set every off-diagonal entry \(x_{ij}, y_{jk}, z_{ki}\) to zero when the two indices differ. Only the terms \(x_{ii}y_{ii}z_{ii}\) survive. Those are \(n\) separate products. So \(\MM(n) \ge n\).

    Approximate squishing

    In 1979, Dario Bini and his coworkers found something strange. Some tasks can be done almost exactly with fewer multiplications than they need exactly. Here is the simplest example. The task

    \[ W = \X{x_0}\Y{y_0}\Z{z_1} + \X{x_0}\Y{y_1}\Z{z_0} + \X{x_1}\Y{y_0}\Z{z_0} \]

    needs 3 multiplications exactly. But for a small number \(\varepsilon\), only 2 pieces give

    \[ \frac{(\X{x_0 + \varepsilon x_1})(\Y{y_0 + \varepsilon y_1})(\Z{z_0 + \varepsilon z_1}) - \X{x_0}\Y{y_0}\Z{z_0}}{\varepsilon} = W + \varepsilon(\cdots) + \varepsilon^2(\cdots). \]

    Look at the powers of \(\varepsilon\) in this example. Before we divide, \(W\) carries \(\varepsilon^1\), and the junk carries \(\varepsilon^2\) and \(\varepsilon^3\). Dividing by \(\varepsilon\) makes \(W\) the lowest power. The paper uses a tidy version of this bookkeeping. Give every variable a whole-number weight. Multiply each variable by \(\varepsilon^{\text{weight}}\). Then each term gets multiplied by \(\varepsilon\) to the power of its total weight. As \(\varepsilon \to 0\), only the terms with the smallest total weight survive. We write \(A \leadsto B\), and we call it a degeneration. Weights can be negative. What matters is that every term has total weight 0 or more, and the terms we want have weight exactly 0. A negative weight does the same job as dividing by \(\varepsilon\). You will see this tool used three times.

    Bini also showed how to remove the \(\varepsilon\). After the squish, the task is a polynomial in \(\varepsilon\), and its constant term is the task we want. If that polynomial has degree \(L\), run the recipe at \(L + 1\) different values of \(\varepsilon\). Then rebuild the constant term from the results, exactly as in Chapter 4. This costs a factor of \(L + 1\) more products. Such factors do not matter, because of the next trick.

    The power trick

    Let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be positive numbers. Suppose you know \(\alpha^N \le 5\cdot \beta^N\) for every whole number \(N\). Take the \(N\)th root: \(\alpha \le 5^{1/N}\cdot \beta\). As \(N\) grows, \(5^{1/N}\) gets closer and closer to 1. So \(\alpha \le \beta\). A fixed constant disappears when you can repeat the argument on bigger and bigger powers.

    The same works for a factor that grows slowly with \(N\). For example, \((5N)^{1/N}\) also gets closer and closer to 1. This covers Bini’s factor, which grows with the power.

    Tasks can be raised to powers with \(\otimes\), and (as the next chapter shows) their scores then get raised to the same power. So this trick shows up everywhere in the proof. Each time a step costs a constant factor (like the 5 above), we raise everything to a big power, and the constant goes away.

    Chapter 6

    Meters: scoring how hard a task is

    Rank is hard to compute. So we look for simpler scores that still respect the rules of the game. A meter \(\lambda\) (the Greek letter lambda) gives each task a number \(\lambda(A) \ge 0\), and it obeys four rules.

    The four meter rules
    1. One product scores 1. \(\lambda(\X{x}\Y{y}\Z{z}) = 1\).
    2. Side by side adds. \(\lambda(A \oplus B) = \lambda(A) + \lambda(B)\).
    3. Together multiplies. \(\lambda(A \otimes B) = \lambda(A)\cdot\lambda(B)\).
    4. Squishing never increases the score. If \(A \ge B\), then \(\lambda(A) \ge \lambda(B)\). This also holds for degenerations \(A \leadsto B\), by Bini’s trick and the power trick.

    Two facts follow quickly. First, \(\lambda(m) = m\) for \(m\) separate products, by rules 1 and 2. Second, a task with rank \(r\) is a squish of \(r\) separate products. So by rule 4:

    \[ \lambda(A) \le \text{rank}(A). \]

    A meter never scores a task above its true cost. Do such meters exist? Yes. Here is the simplest one, called a flattening. Group the terms of a task by their x variable: \(x_1\cdot(\text{rest}_1) + x_2\cdot(\text{rest}_2) + \cdots\). The meter counts the smallest number of “rests” from which you can build every rest by mixing. For example, \(\Dot_X(m)\) has only one x, so it scores 1. In \(\Dot_Y(m) = \Y{y}(\X{x_1}\Z{z_1} + \cdots + \X{x_m}\Z{z_m})\), the rests are \(y z_1, \ldots, y z_m\), and none of them is a mix of the others, so it scores \(m\). Checking the four rules for this meter takes some linear algebra, so we skip that. Other meters are far more complex. In fact, most meters are unknown. The beautiful part: the proof never needs to know what the meters are. It uses only the four rules.

    What a meter says about dot products

    Dot products combine neatly: \(\Dot(m) \otimes \Dot(k) = \Dot(mk)\). A meter turns \(\otimes\) into multiplication, so \(\lambda(\Dot(mk)) = \lambda(\Dot(m))\cdot\lambda(\Dot(k))\). Also, \(\lambda(\Dot(m))\) never decreases as \(m\) grows, because \(\Dot(m+1)\) squishes to \(\Dot(m)\): set \(y_{m+1}\) to zero. A function with both properties must be a power of \(m\) (the fine print below shows why). So there is a number \(p\) between 0 and 1 with

    \[ \lambda(\Dot(m)) = m^{p}. \]

    Each of the three versions of the dot product gets its own exponent: \(p_X\), \(p_Y\), \(p_Z\). And since matrix multiplication is three dot products run together:

    \[ \lambda(\MM(n)) = n^{p_X}\cdot n^{p_Y}\cdot n^{p_Z} = n^{3t}, \qquad t = \frac{p_X + p_Y + p_Z}{3}. \]

    So each meter has one number \(t\), its level, and it says “matrix multiplication costs about \(n^{3t}\)”.

    The level is never zero. From Chapter 5, \(\MM(n) \ge n\), so rule 4 gives \(n^{3t} \ge n\). That means \(3t \ge 1\), so \(t \ge 1/3\).

    Strassen’s bridge

    In 1988 Strassen proved a deep fact. It connects the meters to real recipes. The paper proves the part it needs in its appendix.

    Strassen’s bridge

    If every meter has \(3t \le c\), then \(\omega \le c\).

    Here is one way to think about it. Imagine a market where tasks are goods, and squishing is a legal trade (a harder task can always be traded for an easier one). A meter is a consistent price list for this market. Strassen showed that the price lists tell the whole truth: if every consistent price list says matrix multiplication is cheap, then a cheap recipe really exists. The proof does not build the recipe. That is why the 9/4 result gives no explicit algorithm.

    So our goal is now clear:

    Goal

    Show that every meter has level \(t \le 3/4\). Then every meter says \(\lambda(\MM(n)) \le n^{3 \cdot 3/4} = n^{9/4}\), and Strassen’s bridge gives \(\omega \le 9/4\).

    One more simplification: the score P

    To keep things simple, we assume the meter does not care which leg is which: if you swap two legs of a task, the reading stays the same. We call such a meter balanced. Then the three versions of the dot product score the same, so \(p_X = p_Y = p_Z = t\). (The fine print below explains how the real proof removes this assumption.) Then we define a rescaled score:

    \[ P(A) = \lambda(A)^{1/t}. \]

    Why the strange power? It makes the numbers nice:

    • \(P(\Dot(m)) = (m^t)^{1/t} = m\). A dot product of length \(m\) scores exactly \(m\).
    • \(P(\MM(n)) = (n^{3t})^{1/t} = n^3\). This does not say that matrix multiplication costs \(n^3\). The power \(1/t\) stretches every score in the same way. After the stretch, the meter’s level shows up in one place only: the ceiling below.
    • \(P\) still multiplies under \(\otimes\), and squishing still never increases it.
    • The ceiling becomes \(P(A) \le \text{rank}(A)^{1/t}\).

    For the polynomial task, write \(P(a, b)\) for \(P(\Poly(a, b))\). From Chapter 4, we already know three facts about it:

    Facts we already have

    Rule S (swap). \(P(a,b) = P(b,a)\). The task \(\Poly(b,a)\) is \(\Poly(a,b)\) with the X and Y legs swapped (because \(f \cdot g = g \cdot f\)), and a balanced meter ignores the swap.

    Rule B (base). \(P(1, b) = b\), because \(\Poly(1,b) = \Dot(b)\).

    Rule U (ceiling). \(P(a, b) \le (a + b - 1)^{1/t}\), because the rank is at most \(a + b - 1\).

    The next chapters find two more rules. Those two rules are the new part of the proof.

    Fine print: meters that treat the legs differently

    A real meter can have different \(p_X, p_Y, p_Z\). The paper fixes this with an averaging trick. A meter \(\lambda\) gives six meters, one for each way to reorder the three legs of the task before measuring. The paper multiplies all six readings together and takes the power \(1/(6t)\):

    \[ P(a,b) = \Big(\prod_{\text{6 orders}} \lambda_\pi(\Poly(a,b))\Big)^{1/(6t)}. \]

    Each of \(p_X, p_Y, p_Z\) appears twice among the six reorderings, so the exponents add up to \(6t\). Swapping legs only reorders the six readings, so this combined score does not care which leg is which. The paper does each step on this page for each of the six reorderings and multiplies the results. Because the exponents add up to \(6t\), the result is exactly the step for our balanced \(P\).

    Fine print: why \(\lambda(\Dot(m))\) is a power of \(m\)

    Let \(F(m) = \lambda(\Dot(m))\). Then \(F(mk) = F(m)F(k)\), so \(F(m^N) = F(m)^N\), and \(F\) never decreases. Put \(p = \log_2 F(2)\), so \(F(2^\ell) = 2^{p\ell}\). For any \(m\) and any big power \(N\), choose \(\ell\) with \(2^\ell \le m^N < 2^{\ell+1}\). Then \(2^{p\ell} \le F(m)^N \le 2^{p(\ell + 1)}\). Take \(\log_2\) and divide by \(N\): \(p\ell/N \le \log_2 F(m) \le p(\ell+1)/N\). As \(N\) grows, \(\ell/N\) gets close to \(\log_2 m\). You get \(F(m) = m^p\). Also \(1 \le F(m) \le m\), because \(\Dot(m)\) has rank \(m\) and contains one product. So \(0 \le p \le 1\).

    Fine print: how Strassen’s bridge is proved

    The appendix of the paper argues by contradiction. Suppose no meter reads \(\MM(d)\) as high as some number \(k\) below \(d^{\omega}\). A separation theorem from linear algebra then gives an explicit chain of trades among tasks. Repeating this chain many times would produce a recipe that beats the definition of \(\omega\), which is impossible. A fixed-point theorem (Schauder–Tychonoff) is used to turn an “additive” score into one that also multiplies. This is the most advanced part of the proof. The rest of this page does not depend on its details.

    Chapter 7

    The new trick: separating a shared leg

    This is the central new idea of the paper. Start with a problem.

    Suppose a task splits into pieces \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_M\). Each piece has its own Y variables and its own Z variables. But all the pieces share the same X variables. If the X variables were separate too, the pieces would be side by side, and rule 2 would say: the meter adds them up. With a shared leg, no rule applies. The pieces compete for the same X variables, and the meter could score the whole far below the sum.

    Here is a small example. \(\Dot(M)\) is \(M\) single products that share one x. If the x’s were separate, the meter would read \(M\). With the shared x, it reads only \(M^t\), which is less than \(M\) when \(t < 1\). So in \(\lambda\), a shared leg costs something. Now look at the rescaled score: \(P(\Dot(M)) = M\), which is exactly the sum of the scores of \(M\) single products. The machine below shows that the same thing happens for every task with a shared leg.

    The paper builds a machine that fixes this. It takes \(5M\) copies of the task and produces \(M\) pieces that are fully separate. And each piece comes with a free bonus: an extra dot product of length \(M\).

    \[ 5M \text{ copies of } A \;\leadsto\; \big(A_1 \otimes \Dot(M)\big) \oplus \big(A_2 \otimes \Dot(M)\big) \oplus \cdots \oplus \big(A_M \otimes \Dot(M)\big). \]

    The machine has two parts: a clock filter and a squish with clever weights.

    Part 1: the clock filter

    Draw \(L\) arrows of length 1, head to tail. Arrow number \(r\) points at angle \(r \cdot k \cdot \frac{360^\circ}{L}\). If \(k\) is a multiple of \(L\), every arrow points the same way, and the total is \(L\). For any other \(k\), the arrows walk around a closed loop and end where they started. The total is exactly 0.

    Why? Turn the whole picture by \(k \cdot 360^\circ / L\). Each arrow lands exactly on the next one, and the last one lands on the first. So the set of arrows is the same, and the sum is the same. But turning also turns the sum. A turn of less than a full circle can leave an arrow unchanged only if that arrow has length zero. So the sum is zero.

    These arrows are complex numbers: arrow \(r\) is \(\zeta^{rk}\), where \(\zeta\) is a complex number that turns by \(360^\circ/L\). This is why the theorem is stated over the complex numbers. The sum works as a filter. It keeps a term only if a certain number \(k\) is zero.

    Here is how the machine uses it. Take \(L = 5M\) copies of the task, numbered \(r = 0, 1, \ldots, L-1\). Each copy has its own variables. The squish replaces them with mixes of the same new variables. So after the squish, all copies live on one set of variables, and they add up into one big sum.

    The new variables are these. Each shared x gets \(M\) new versions \(\X{X_g}\), with a “guess label” \(g = 1, \ldots, M\). The guess says which piece this x might belong to. Each y in piece \(h\) gets versions \(\Y{Y_{h,u}}\), and each z in piece \(h\) gets versions \(\Z{Z_{h,v}}\), with extra labels \(u, v = 1, \ldots, M\). In copy \(r\), each new variable also carries a clock arrow:

    \[ \X{X_g} \text{ gets } \zeta^{2rg}, \qquad \Y{Y_{h,u}} \text{ gets } \zeta^{r(u-h)}, \qquad \Z{Z_{h,v}} \text{ gets } \zeta^{-r(v+h)}. \]

    Each leg uses only the labels it knows. Multiplying arrows of length 1 adds their angles. So a term with labels \(g, h, u, v\) gets the arrow \(\zeta^{rk}\), where

    \[ k = 2g + (u - h) - (v + h) = u - v + 2(g - h). \]

    Adding up all \(L\) copies keeps exactly the terms with \(k = 0\). (The labels run from 1 to \(M\), so \(k\) lies between \(-3(M-1)\) and \(3(M-1)\). Any \(L\) above \(3(M-1)\) makes sure that \(k\) is never a nonzero multiple of \(L\). The paper picks \(L = 5M\). The z variables also carry a factor \(1/L\), so each kept term appears exactly once.)

    Part 2: the squish with a perfect square

    We want only terms with a correct guess, \(g = h\). So we want each term to get weight \((g - h)^2\). Then the right guesses have weight 0, and every wrong guess has weight at least 1 and disappears.

    Here is the difficulty. A weight must be given to each variable on its own. \(\X{X_g}\) knows \(g\) but not \(h\). \(\Y{Y_{h,u}}\) and \(\Z{Z_{h,v}}\) know \(h\) but not \(g\). No single leg can compare \(g\) with \(h\). Expand the square: \((g-h)^2 = g^2 - 2gh + h^2\). X can carry \(g^2\). The cross term \(-2gh\) is the hard part, because it mixes \(g\) and \(h\). But after the filter, \(u - v = -2(g-h)\). Multiply by \(h\): \(h(u - v) = -2gh + 2h^2\). So \(-2gh + h^2 = hu - hv - h^2\), and this uses only labels that Y and Z know. That gives the paper’s weights:

    \[ w(\X{X_g}) = g^2, \qquad w(\Y{Y_{h,u}}) = hu - h^2, \qquad w(\Z{Z_{h,v}}) = -hv. \]

    A term that passed the filter has \(u - v = -2(g - h)\). So its total weight is

    \[ g^2 + hu - h^2 - hv \;=\; g^2 - h^2 + h(u - v) \;=\; g^2 - h^2 - 2h(g-h) \;=\; (g - h)^2. \]

    A perfect square. The clock filter ties \(u - v\) to \(g - h\), and that lets the three legs share the job of computing \((g-h)^2\). When \(g = h\), the filter also forces \(u = v\). The surviving terms are \(\X{X_h}\,\Y{Y_{h,u}}\,\Z{Z_{h,u}}\): each piece \(h\) now has its own x variables, and the free index \(u\) gives a bonus \(\Dot(M)\).

    What the machine gives the meter

    Suppose all \(M\) pieces are the same. Apply the meter to both ends of the machine. On the left, \(5M\) separate copies score \(5M\cdot\lambda(A)\) (rule 2). On the right, \(M\) separate pieces, each times \(\Dot(M)\), score \(M\cdot M^{t}\cdot\lambda(A_1)\) (rules 2 and 3). A squish never increases the score, so

    \[ 5M\cdot\lambda(A) \;\ge\; M\cdot M^{t}\cdot\lambda(A_1). \]

    Cancel \(M\), then raise both sides to the power \(1/t\) to switch to the score \(P\):

    \[ 5^{1/t}\cdot P(A) \;\ge\; M\cdot P(A_1). \]

    Now the power trick removes the constant \(5^{1/t}\). Apply the same machine to \(A \otimes A \otimes \cdots \otimes A\) (\(N\) times). That task also shares its X leg, and it has \(M^N\) equal pieces, each equal to \(A_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes A_1\). The machine gives \(5^{1/t}\cdot P(A)^N \ge M^N \cdot P(A_1)^N\). Take \(N\)th roots and let \(N\) grow. So \(P(A) \ge M\cdot P(A_1)\): the score of the whole is at least the sum of the scores of its \(M\) pieces. Look at what happened. The factor \(M^t\) from the bonus dot product paid back the cost of the copies. Without the bonus, the machine would be useless.

    The shared-leg rule

    If a task degenerates into pieces that share only the X leg (with separate Y and separate Z variables), then

    \[ P(\text{task}) \ge P(\text{piece}_1) + P(\text{piece}_2) + \cdots \]

    A shared leg costs nothing, as far as the score \(P\) can tell.

    The text above proves this for equal pieces. Chapter 8 needs two pieces of different sizes. The box below shows how the paper handles that case.

    Deeper look: pieces of different sizes

    When the two pieces have different scores \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), the paper raises the task to a big power \(N\). Each term of the power picks a piece in each of its \(N\) factors, like a word of length \(N\) made of two letters, one letter for each kind of piece. Keep only the words with a fixed fraction \(q\) of the first letter. Those words form many equal pieces that share the X leg. The number of such words is about \(e^{N H(q)}\), where \(H(q) = -q\ln q - (1-q)\ln(1-q)\) is the entropy. For example, with \(q = 1/2\) the count is the binomial coefficient \(\binom{N}{N/2}\). It is close to \(2^N = e^{N \ln 2}\) (up to a much smaller factor), and \(H(1/2) = \ln 2\). The machine then gives

    \[ P(\text{task}) \ge e^{H(q)}\,\alpha^{q}\,\beta^{1-q} \quad\text{for every } q. \]

    Now choose the best \(q\). Try it below: the best value is always exactly \(\alpha + \beta\), at \(q = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\).

    Chapter 8

    Rule C: the score bends

    The first new rule is about how \(P(a, b)\) changes when \(b\) grows by one step at a time.

    Rule C (bending) \[ 2P(a, b) \;\ge\; P(a, b+1) + P(a, b-1) \qquad (b \ge 2). \]

    Move the terms around: \(P(a,b) - P(a,b-1) \ge P(a,b+1) - P(a,b)\). Each step up in \(b\) gains no more than the step before it. The graph of \(P\) bends downward, like a hill that gets flatter.

    Here is the proof idea in four moves.

    Move 1: do the task twice

    Multiply \(f\) by two polynomials \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) at once, each with \(b\) coefficients. This is \(\Poly(a, b) \otimes \Dot_X(2)\): the input \(f\) is shared, so it plays the lone X role of the dot product. Its score is \(2P(a, b)\), since \(P\) multiplies and \(P(\Dot_X(2)) = 2\).

    Move 2: a pair is one long plus one short

    A pair \((g_1, g_2)\) holds \(2b\) numbers. A polynomial of length \(b + 1\) and one of length \(b - 1\) together also hold \(2b\) numbers. The trick is to cut the pair into those two parts. Send the pair to one polynomial:

    \[ (g_1, g_2) \;\longmapsto\; s\cdot g_1 + g_2. \]

    The result has \(b + 1\) coefficients. Now pick \(2b\) special pairs:

    • Long pairs: \((0, 1), (1, 0), (s, 0), \ldots, (s^{b-1}, 0)\). They map to \(1, s, s^2, \ldots, s^b\). There are \(b + 1\) of them.
    • Short pairs: \((1, -s), (s, -s^2), \ldots, (s^{b-2}, -s^{b-1})\). They all map to 0. There are \(b - 1\) of them.

    Every pair is a mix of these \(2b\) special pairs in exactly one way. Try it for \(b = 2\): the pair \((c_0 + c_1 s,\; d_0 + d_1 s)\) equals \(d_0\cdot(0,1) + (c_0 + d_1)\cdot(1,0) + c_1 \cdot (s, 0) - d_1\cdot(1, -s)\). Expand it to check. The mix numbers are new variables for the Y leg. Switching to them is a squish, and another squish switches back. So by rule 4, the switch does not change any meter reading. We do the same for the output pairs on the Z leg.

    Move 3: multiplying by f respects the split

    Multiply the pair by \(f\). Then \(s\cdot(fg_1) + fg_2 = f\cdot(s g_1 + g_2)\). So on the long part, it is plain multiplication by \(f\): that is \(\Poly(a, b+1)\). A short pair has the form \((c, -s\cdot c)\), and \(f\) sends it to \((fc, -s\cdot fc)\), which is again a short pair. On the short part, it is again multiplication by \(f\): that is \(\Poly(a, b-1)\).

    One more point matters. The special pairs do not depend on \(f\). The coefficients of \(f\) form the shared X leg, so one choice of new variables must work for every \(f\) at once. It does.

    Write the task as a table in the new variables. The long and short parts give two diagonal blocks. One corner is zero, because short pairs go to short pairs. The other corner (marked \(*\)) has a few leftover entries. They come from the long pair \((0, 1)\): \(f\cdot(0,1) = (0, f)\), and \((0, f)\) has some short-pair parts.

    Move 4: squish, then use the shared-leg rule

    Give weight \(-1\) to the input short-part variables, weight \(+1\) to the output short-part labels, and 0 to everything else. The two diagonal blocks get weight \(-1 + 1 = 0\) or \(0 + 0 = 0\), so they stay. The leftover corner gets weight \(0 + 1 = 1\), so it disappears. The other corner would get weight \(-1\), but it is empty. So

    \[ \Poly(a, b) \otimes \Dot(2) \;\leadsto\; \Poly(a, b+1) \text{ and } \Poly(a, b-1), \]

    two pieces that share the X leg (the coefficients of \(f\)) and have separate Y and Z variables. The shared-leg rule gives \(2P(a, b) \ge P(a, b+1) + P(a, b-1)\). That is Rule C.

    By Rule S, the same bending holds in the first variable \(a\) too.

    Chapter 9

    Rule T: tripling

    Rule T (tripling) \[ P(a,\; 3h + a - 1) \;\ge\; 3\,P(a, h). \]

    For all \(a, h \ge 1\): a long polynomial task contains three copies of a shorter one. The extra \(a - 1\) in the length is a small “tax”.

    Draw \(\Poly(a, B)\) as a grid. Rows are the Y variables \(y_0, y_1, \ldots\). Columns are the Z labels \(z_0, z_1, \ldots\). The term \(\X{x_i}\Y{y_j}\Z{z_{i+j}}\) sits in row \(j\), column \(i + j\). So each \(x_i\) fills a slanted stripe.

    Take \(B = 3h + a - 1\). Row \(j\) has terms in columns \(j\) to \(j + a - 1\). Now cut the rows into three groups (first, middle, last), and the columns too:

    • Rows: \(0\) to \(h-1\); then \(h\) to \(2h+a-2\); then \(2h+a-1\) to \(3h+a-2\).
    • Columns: \(0\) to \(h+a-2\); then \(h+a-1\) to \(2h+a-2\); then \(2h+a-1\) to \(3h+2a-3\).

    Give weight \(+1\) to the middle rows (Y variables) and weight \(-1\) to the middle columns (Z labels). Watch what survives.

    Three blocks remain. They share the X leg (the same \(x_0, \ldots, x_{a-1}\)), and they have separate rows and separate columns.

    • The first and last blocks are copies of \(\Poly(a, h)\), just shifted.
    • The middle block is \(\Poly(a, h)\) with the Y and Z legs swapped. Its Y side is the long one. To match \(\Poly(a,h)\) exactly, also rename \(x_i\) as \(x_{a-1-i}\) (we do this only to identify this one block). A balanced meter ignores the leg swap, so this block scores \(P(a, h)\) too.

    The shared-leg rule gives \(P(a, 3h + a - 1) \ge 3P(a, h)\). That is Rule T.

    Why the tax of \(a - 1\)? The middle block needs room. Its Y side has \(h + a - 1\) rows, longer than the outer blocks. That extra room is the price of the swap, and this price is the reason the final answer is 9/4 and not 2.

    Why three blocks and not two? Two blocks also fit. Keep the first block, skip \(a - 1\) rows, and put a second copy after them. That gives \(P(a, 2h + a - 1) \ge 2P(a, h)\), but the \(a - 1\) skipped rows go to waste. With only two copies, the steps of the next chapter would give only \(P(a,a) \ge\) about \(a\), and that leads to \(\omega \le 3\), which we knew already. The swapped middle block fills the gap and adds a third copy for the same tax.

    Chapter 10

    The squeeze

    We now have five rules for the score \(P(a, b)\) of any meter:

    The five rules

    S   \(P(a,b) = P(b,a)\)

    B   \(P(1,b) = b\)

    U   \(P(a,b) \le (a+b-1)^{1/t}\)

    C   \(2P(a,b) \ge P(a,b+1) + P(a,b-1)\)

    T   \(P(a, 3h+a-1) \ge 3P(a,h)\)

    Only rule U mentions the meter’s level \(t\). The others hold for every meter. The plan: rules S, B, C and T force \(P\) to grow fast along the diagonal \(a = b\). Rule U says it cannot grow too fast. Squeeze the two together.

    Step 1: the slope at the diagonal

    Fix \(a\) and write \(D_a = P(a, a)\). Use rule T with \(h = a\): \(P(a, 4a - 1) \ge 3D_a\). So as \(b\) goes from \(a\) to \(4a - 1\) (that is \(3a - 1\) steps), \(P\) gains at least \(2D_a\). The average step is at least \(\frac{2D_a}{3a-1}\). By rule C, the steps only get smaller. So the first step is at least the average. We give this number a name:

    \[ P(a, a+1) - P(a, a) \;\ge\; H_a, \qquad H_a = \frac{2D_a}{3a - 1}. \]

    Step 2: walk up the diagonal

    To get from \(P(a-1, a-1)\) to \(P(a, a)\), take two steps:

    • From \((a-1, a-1)\) to \((a-1, a)\). This is the first step after the diagonal in row \(a - 1\). By Step 1, it is at least \(H_{a-1}\).
    • From \((a-1, a)\) to \((a, a)\). By rule S, this equals the step from \((a, a-1)\) to \((a, a)\). That step comes just before the first step after the diagonal in row \(a\). By rule C, a step is never smaller than the step after it. So it is at least \(H_a\).
    \[ D_a - D_{a-1} \;\ge\; H_{a-1} + H_a. \]

    In words: each diagonal step gains about \(2 \times \frac{2D_a}{3a} = \frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{D_a}{a}\). That is the source of the 4/3: two directions (the 2) times the tripling slope (the \(2/3\)).

    Step 3: from 4/3 per step to \(a^{4/3}\)

    First, a quick check of why this means \(a^{4/3}\). Suppose \(D_a = a^{4/3}\). Then \(D_{101}/D_{100} = (1.01)^{4/3} \approx 1.0133\). So the step from 100 to 101 gains about \(0.0133\cdot D_{100}\), which is \(\frac{4/3}{100}\cdot D_{100}\). That matches \(\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{D_a}{a}\). Now the exact argument. It uses only algebra:

    1. Put \(D_a = \frac{3a-1}{2}H_a\) into the step inequality and collect terms. You get \(H_a \ge \big(1 + \frac{1}{3(a-1)}\big)H_{a-1}\).
    2. Start from \(D_1 = P(1,1) = 1\), so \(H_1 = 1\). Repeat the step: \(H_a \ge \big(1 + \frac{1}{3}\big)\big(1 + \frac{1}{6}\big)\cdots\big(1 + \frac{1}{3(a-1)}\big)\).
    3. Expand a cube: \(\big(1 + \frac{1}{3m}\big)^3 = 1 + \frac1m + \frac{1}{3m^2} + \frac{1}{27m^3} \ge 1 + \frac1m = \frac{m+1}{m}\).
    4. So \(H_a^3 \ge \frac21\cdot\frac32\cdot\frac43\cdots\frac{a}{a-1} = a\), because everything cancels except \(a\). So \(H_a \ge a^{1/3}\).
    5. Finally, \(D_a = \frac{3a-1}{2}H_a \ge a\cdot a^{1/3}\), because \(\frac{3a-1}{2} \ge a\).
    \[ P(a, a) \;\ge\; a^{4/3} \quad\text{for every } a. \]

    Step 4: squeeze

    Now bring in rule U with \(b = a\):

    \[ a^{4/3} \;\le\; P(a,a) \;\le\; (2a - 1)^{1/t}. \]

    The left side grows like \(a^{4/3}\). The right side grows like \(a^{1/t}\). If \(1/t\) were smaller than \(4/3\), the left side would win for large \(a\). That would be a contradiction. Try it:

    So every meter has \(1/t \ge 4/3\), which means \(t \le 3/4\). Every meter scores matrix multiplication at

    \[ \lambda(\MM(n)) = n^{3t} \le n^{3\cdot 3/4} = n^{9/4}. \]

    By Strassen’s bridge:

    ω ≤ 9/4

    What does this give in practice? Strassen’s bridge, in the form the paper proves, says: for any tiny \(\varepsilon\), there is some block size \(u\) and a recipe for \(u \times u\) matrices with fewer than \(u^{9/4+\varepsilon}\) products. The recursion rule from Chapter 2 turns that recipe into a method with about \(n^{9/4+\varepsilon}\) steps. Nobody knows \(u\) or the recipe.

    Chapter 11

    The anatomy of 9/4

    Every part of the number now has a meaning:

    \[ \omega \;\le\; 3 \times \frac{\text{ceiling growth}}{\text{floor growth}} \;=\; 3 \times \frac{1}{4/3} \;=\; \frac{9}{4}. \]
    3
    Matrix multiplication is three dot products, one per pair of legs (Chapter 3).
    1
    Multiplying polynomials costs \(2a - 1\): a straight line (Chapter 4).
    2
    Each diagonal step moves in both \(a\) and \(b\) (Chapter 10).
    2/3
    Tripling gains 2 extra copies over about \(3a\) steps (Chapter 9).

    The floor growth is \(2 \times 2/3 = 4/3\). The tax of \(a - 1\) in rule T is what makes the tripling slope \(2/3\). Here is a thought experiment (it is not in the paper). Suppose the tax were \(\tau a\) for some number \(\tau\). Then the same steps would give a floor growth of \(4/(2+\tau)\) and a bound of \(\omega \le 3(2 + \tau)/4\).

    Test the rules yourself

    The five rules are strong. Below, three candidate score tables are checked against all five rules for many values of \(a\) and \(b\).

    • Naive guess. \(P = a + b - 1\) is what a meter of level \(t = 1\) would need (the school method). It breaks rule T already at \(a = 2, h = 1\): \(P(2, 4) = 5\) but \(3P(2, 1) = 6\). So no meter can have this table as its score. (To rule out every meter of level 1, you still need the full squeeze.)
    • Flattening meter. A real meter with \(p_X = 0, p_Y = p_Z = 1\), so \(t = 2/3\). On \(\Poly(a,b)\), its three versions (one per leg) count \(a\), \(b\) and \(a+b-1\). Its averaged score (from the fine print in Chapter 6) is the square root of their product, \(\sqrt{ab(a+b-1)}\). It passes every rule, as it must.
    • Extremal P*. A score table found after the paper by independent researchers. It passes all five rules with \(1/t = 4/3\). Its diagonal values are exactly the green dots of Chapter 10, the lowest values that the steps allow. So these five rules alone can never prove anything better than 9/4. (This table comes from community work after the paper. It is not part of the paper.)

    Chapter 12

    The full picture

    Goal. Show \(\omega \le 9/4\).
    ↓
    Strassen’s bridge. It is enough to show that every meter has level \(t \le 3/4\), because \(\lambda(\MM(n)) = n^{3t}\).
    ↓
    Point the meter at polynomials. Score \(P(a,b)\) of \(\Poly(a,b)\).
    ↓
    Ceiling. Evaluation trick: rank \(2a-1\), so \(P(a,a) \le (2a-1)^{1/t}\).
    Floor. Shared-leg machine → rules C and T → \(P(a,a) \ge a^{4/3}\).
    ↓
    Squeeze. \(a^{4/3} \le (2a-1)^{1/t}\) for all \(a\), so \(t \le 3/4\).
    ↓
    Result. \(\omega \le 3 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac94\).

    Now go back to the five steps at the top of the page. Each one should make sense.

    What the result does and does not give

    • No recipe in hand. The proof shows that fast recipes exist for some huge block size. It does not write one down, and their hidden constants are probably enormous. Your computer will not get faster tomorrow.
    • Checked by computer. The OpenAI repository includes a formal proof in Lean (a computer proof checker) of the statement \(\omega \le 9/4\) over the complex numbers. As of October 2026, the paper has not gone through peer review. Community projects on GitHub report that they rebuilt the Lean proof independently, and that they extended the result to other number systems. These are also unreviewed.
    • Where next. Since 2 is the floor, the gap is now between 2 and 2.25. The extremal table P* shows that new rules are needed to go further.
